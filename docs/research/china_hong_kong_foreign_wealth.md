# Mainland China and Hong Kong SAR as a long-term home and wealth base for a wealthy foreign family

Date: 2026-10-15. Scope: non-Chinese (EU/US/other) family, about EUR 30M liquid portfolio.
Method: every claim below is tied to a page that was opened in this research session (numbered sources at the end).
Where a primary source could not be opened, the text says so and uses a labelled secondary source. This is research, not legal or tax advice.

## 1. Mainland China

### 1.1 Capital controls
- IMF (Country Report 26/44, 2025 Article IV, Feb 2026): China accepted Article VIII, so there are no restrictions on payments and transfers for *current* international transactions (except notified security restrictions). "While exchange controls continue to apply to most capital transactions" (S3). The same report lists the QDII outbound quota as an "outflow CFM" (capital flow management measure) that was eased in June 2025 (S3).
- SAFE: the individual annual settlement/purchase quota is USD 50,000 (S5). Per a China Briefing explainer (2015, dated), the USD 50,000 cap is described as applying to Chinese citizens, and foreigners remit lawfully earned RMB by showing the bank evidence that it was legally obtained and taxed: labour contract, work permit, pay slips, tax certificate (S6). Without papers, only about USD 500 a day (S6, 2015 rule, may have changed).
- Property sale proceeds: foreign individuals can convert RMB sale proceeds only with approval of the foreign exchange bureau, on a sales agreement and proof of all taxes paid (S7, 2013). A practitioner post says transfers of RMB 500,000 or more need SAFE approval and that a co-owner not on the title deed may not be recognised (S8, 2012 post, comments to 2025).
- Reading: a resident foreigner can usually move *documented* salary and sale proceeds out, but only with paperwork, approval steps and tax clearance, and with no right to move savings or investment capital freely. The state can refuse or slow it down. Capital-account transactions remain under exchange controls per the IMF.

### 1.2 Property rights
- All land is state-owned; the owner holds a land-use right. Residential terms are 70 years, commercial/tourism/entertainment 40 years (S9, National Immigration Administration page). The Civil Code (art. 359) says residential rights "will be automatically renewed"; whether a renewal fee is due is left to later rules (S9, S10: Withers, Sept 2020). Non-residential renewal is less certain (search summary only; not verified by an opened page).
- Foreign individuals may buy residential property only for self-use, only **one** dwelling, with at least one year of work or study in China, unless they are branch/representative-office staff or Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan or overseas-Chinese residents (S7, 2013, dated). The buyer signs an undertaking that he owns no other home in China (S7). A 2026 consumer guide reports no material change since (search result only, not opened).
- Expropriation compensation text of the Civil Code was not opened; no assertion made.

### 1.3 Rule of law and courts
- WJP Rule of Law Index 2025: China rank **92 of 143, score 0.48** (WJP report PDF, S1). Hong Kong **24 of 143, 0.72**. China is 12th of 15 in East Asia and Pacific (S1).
- Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026: China **9/100, Not Free**; independent judiciary 1/4, because courts at all levels are supervised by party political-legal committees; due process 1/4 (S11).
- Counter-espionage law lets authorities stop people from leaving or entering on national security grounds (S11).

### 1.4 Personal risk
- US State Department: China Level 2, "Exercise Increased Caution", reissued early September 2026 (reported 4 Sept by one search summary, 5 Sept by S14, 8 Sept by Fox S15). Reasons: arbitrary enforcement of local laws, exit bans without a fair and transparent process, risk of unjust arrest or detention. Business people, academics and journalists have been questioned, detained or expelled over alleged national security violations; some exit bans lasted years (S14, S15). Second-hand: official page returned 403.
- UK FCDO (China safety and security page, opened 2026-10-15): exit bans "can be put on people involved in commercial or private disputes"; they last "months or years or until a legal process is complete"; one may learn of one only on trying to leave; "British nationals have been detained against their will, intimidated and forced to pay money"; "you can be arrested without having intended to break the law" (S12).
- New exit/entry regulation (State Council Order 841, passed 31 July 2026, in force 15 Sept 2026): exit bans for citizens with waivable notification; foreign nationals on countermeasure, unreliable-entity or malicious-entity lists, or under PRC sanctions, may be refused exit or entry documents; foreign sponsor, executive or affiliate exposure (S16 Safeguard Defenders, 3 Aug 2026; S17 Brownstein, 17 Sept 2026). Safeguard Defenders is an advocacy NGO; Brownstein is a law firm.

### 1.5 Sanctions and geopolitical exposure
- Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law implementing regulations (State Council, 23 March 2025): scope widened to harm to China's "sovereignty, security, and development interests"; listed foreign persons, including individuals, can face seizure, detention or freezing of real property, bank deposits, securities, fund shares, equity and other assets in China, and limits on work permit or residence; Chinese persons are encouraged to sue anyone helping implement foreign discriminatory measures, and enforcement of judgments harming China's interests is barred (S18 Hughes Hubbard, 14 Apr 2025).
- Consequence for a Western family: complying with US/EU sanctions (e.g. through a bank or fund) is the trigger risk, not misconduct.

### 1.6 Other material points
- Tax: a foreigner resident 183+ days in each of six consecutive years becomes taxable on worldwide income; a single absence over 30 consecutive days restarts the count; counting started 1 Jan 2019, so first exposure was 2025 (S19, China Briefing, March 2019 and so dated).
- Data/CRS/banking access: not opened, not asserted.

## 2. Hong Kong SAR

### 2.1 Capital controls
- Basic Law art. 112 (S20): "No foreign exchange control policies shall be applied", the Hong Kong dollar is "freely convertible", and the government "shall safeguard the free flow of capital within, into and out of the Region". Art. 105 protects acquisition, use, disposal and inheritance of property and compensation at "real value" for lawful deprivation (S20).
- HK Government portal: "no foreign exchange controls, allowing for the free movement of capital in and out of the region"; currency freely convertible (S21).
- IMF Article IV, Jan 2025 and May 2026: the Linked Exchange Rate System "remains appropriate and continues to serve as a credible anchor" (S22, S23). The pages opened do not discuss capital flows.
- Financial Secretary Paul Chan, 1 June 2020: the national security law would not restrict capital flows or impose controls (S24). Dated, a political assurance rather than a legal guarantee.
- Countervailing fact: the NSL framework lets the government **freeze assets of national-security suspects until proceedings end**; the two-year cap was removed in December 2023, and bank accounts of former lawmakers and civil-society groups were frozen (S25, OFAC/State advisory, 6 Sept 2024).

### 2.2 Property rights
- No foreigner restriction found in the sources opened. Since 28 February 2024 all demand-side stamp duties (including the 15% Buyer's Stamp Duty on non-permanent residents) are abolished; non-permanent residents now pay the same ad valorem duty as permanent residents (S26 Chambers Private Wealth 2026). Property tax is 15% of net assessable rental value (S26). The 2026/27 Budget proposed a higher rate for homes above HKD 100M (S26; enactment not verified).
- US advisory (S25): Hong Kong retains "well-protected property ownership and intellectual property rights" and a separate common-law system for commercial and civil litigation.

### 2.3 Rule of law and courts
- WJP 2025: Hong Kong **24/143, 0.72**, change -0.5% (S1; also HK government release S2). Reported rank is government-framed in S2 but the figures match the WJP PDF.
- Freedom House 2026: Hong Kong **41/100, Partly Free** (political rights 9/40) (S27). The NSL was imposed by the NPC bypassing LegCo and courts; the chief executive picks the judges for NSL cases; bail denied in about 90% of NSL cases in 2025 and conviction rate about 87% (Amnesty figures cited by FH); Article 23 ordinance (2024) allows detention without charge for up to 16 days (S27).
- National Security Law (2020) and Safeguarding National Security Ordinance (Article 23, March 2024): broad offences ("colluding with external forces", state secrets, espionage, sedition), with extraterritorial reach, including state-secrets offences for anyone outside Hong Kong irrespective of nationality, and a definition of national security covering "the welfare of the people" and "other interests of the state" (S25). Maximum penalties up to life imprisonment (S28 FCDO, S25).
- Overseas judges: Spigelman left 2020; Reed and Hodge resigned March 2022; Sumption and Collins resigned announced 6 June 2024 (S29 RFA). FH: six foreign jurists have stepped down since 2024; six still served at end 2025 (S27).
- Independence in non-political matters: a High Court judge ordered China Evergrande liquidated on 29 January 2024 (S30, AFP); in March 2025 the Court of Final Appeal quashed 2023 convictions of three Tiananmen vigil organisers (S27). Jimmy Lai received 20 years on 9 February 2026 (S31, Reuters) after conviction in December 2025 (S27). Commercial courts still function; the political-security domain does not.

### 2.4 Personal risk
- US State Department: Hong Kong Level 2, "Exercise Increased Caution" because of arbitrary enforcement of local laws; reissued early September 2026; no exit-ban or detention language reported (S14 via secondary source, S15). Second-hand.
- UK FCDO (updated 1 April 2026; opened 2026-10-15): national security laws "can be interpreted broadly", apply "to all individuals regardless of nationality or residency", inside and outside Hong Kong including the UK; "you could be detained or removed to mainland China for some offences"; arrest warrants and rewards were issued against people abroad; people have been prosecuted for statements critical of the authorities (S28). Hong Kong "does not recognise dual nationality" (S28 main page).
- US advisory (S25): authorities arrested over 200 people under NSL provisions since 2020 and charged more than 100; the six people charged under the Article 23 ordinance concerned Facebook posts.

### 2.5 Sanctions and geopolitical exposure
- US sanctions on Hong Kong officials: a 31 March 2025 State Department action designating six officials (including Secretary for Justice Paul Lam and Police Commissioner Raymond Siu) for transnational repression was reported in search results; the State Department page itself could not be opened (HTTP 402 via the mirror in S33), so treat as unverified. The August 2026 congressional letter (S34) states the Administration continues to treat Hong Kong as "no longer sufficiently autonomous" for differential treatment under US law.
- A 14 August 2026 letter from members of Congress states that the national emergency under EO 13936 lapsed on 14 July 2026, that 9 of 48 individuals were delisted on 17 July 2026 and the other 39 remain under the Hong Kong Autonomy Act (S34). This is a legislators' letter, not an OFAC page; treat as unverified until OFAC's notice is read.
- PRC Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law in Hong Kong: **not established.** Search summaries (not opened pages) indicate that adding it to Basic Law Annex III was postponed in August 2021 and that, as of 2024 and 2025, it had not been adopted in Hong Kong; I could not open a source confirming 2025-2026 status (the Debevoise 2021 note, S35, was not opened either). The Sept 2024 US advisory (S25) does not discuss it in the text I read. HKMA guidance to banks on foreign unilateral sanctions was likewise only seen in a search summary. Treat Hong Kong AFSL exposure as an open question for counsel.
- Freezing risk: asset freezes against national-security suspects are legal (S25).

### 2.6 Other
- No capital gains, withholding or estate tax in Hong Kong (Charles Russell guide via search summary, not opened); Chambers (S26) confirms property tax only.
- Banking: HKMA statements in June 2026 about stricter checks on mainland applicants (search summary only; not opened).

## 3. Verdicts

**Mainland China: knock-out.** An IMF-documented capital-account control regime (exchange controls on most capital transactions, S3), approval-based conversion of property proceeds for foreigners (S7), a one-home / one-year residence rule, land held as leasehold (S9), WJP 92/143 and FH 9/100 (S1, S11), exit bans that are officially said to be used in commercial disputes (S12), a US Level 2 advisory that names exit bans and unjust detention (S14, S15), and legal powers to freeze a listed foreigner's bank deposits, securities and real estate (S18) together mean the wealth cannot be assumed freely movable or safe. A family could in principle live there on a portfolio held fully offshore, but that is a different (and still exposed) proposition than making China a wealth base.

**Hong Kong SAR: red flag, not a knock-out on capital movement, but near-knock-out on legal-security grounds for individuals.** Money can move freely under Basic Law art. 112 and the currency board peg (S20-S23), property is open to foreigners at the same stamp duty as locals (S26), commercial courts still decide market cases (S30). Against that: WJP stays high but FH has fallen to 41 (S27), the NSL and Article 23 ordinance create extraterritorial, vaguely drawn offences for any nationality (S25, S28), assets of suspects can be frozen without a time limit (S25), and the overseas judge bench has thinned (S27, S29). It is acceptable only as a place to **bank and hold** assets, with strict rules on conduct, data, and travel; as a long-term family home the national-security exposure is a flag that the model should penalise heavily.

## 4. Proposed objective criteria for the same rule in other countries

1. **Knock-out (capital):** the country applies individual capital controls that cap or require approval for moving personal wealth abroad. Test: the latest IMF Article IV staff report or AREAER states that exchange controls apply to most capital transactions *and* resident individuals' outward transfers are subject to a quota or case-by-case approval. Source evidence for China: IMF Country Report 26/44 (S3) and SAFE's USD 50,000 quota (S5). Hong Kong passes this test (S20, S21).
2. **Red flag (security of person and assets):** the government's own travel advisory or the FCDO describes exit bans, arbitrary detention of foreigners, or asset freezes by executive action. Hong Kong triggers the asset-freeze and arbitrary-enforcement strand (S25, S28); China triggers all strands (S12, S14).
3. **Supporting score screens:** WJP Rule of Law Index 2025 overall below 0.50 or Freedom House Not Free as a flag; both are met by China (0.48, 9/100), neither by Hong Kong on WJP (0.72) but Hong Kong is Partly Free (41/100).
