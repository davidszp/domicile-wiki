# Macau SAR as a long-term home and wealth base for a wealthy foreign family, and the `security_state_courts` question

Date: 2026-10-16. Scope: non-Chinese (EU/US/other) family, about EUR 30M liquid portfolio.
Method: every claim below is tied to a page that was opened in this research session (numbered sources at the end).
Where a primary source could not be opened, the text says so and uses a labelled secondary source. This is research, not legal or tax advice.
Question: does Macau meet the definition of `security_state_courts` ("A national-security regime that can override ordinary judicial protections, freeze assets without time limit and reach any nationality, documented by government advisories. Courts may still decide ordinary commercial cases, but property and liberty are not reliably protected against the state.") as fully as Hong Kong (see docs/research/china_hong_kong_foreign_wealth.md)?

## Reading limits (read first)

- travel.state.gov returned HTTP 403 (Cloudflare) for the Macau advisory page, so the US advisory level and wording are **second-hand** (S14, S15). A search result snippet attributed to the official page also says Level 3, issued 4 September 2026 (search summary only, not opened).
- The State Department 2025 Investment Climate Statement for Macau (state.gov HTML and PDF) returned HTTP 403, and the 2024 edition rendered only a "Technical Difficulties" shell. Its statements on capital, expropriation and the national security law are therefore **not used**, except where quoted in a search summary and marked as such.
- The official text of Law 2/2009 as amended in 2023 (Official Gazette / Legislative Assembly) could not be opened. Its scope, extraterritoriality and procedure come from news agencies, HRW and the 2009 text as reproduced by CECC (S9). The amended article numbers are not verified.
- The 2026 Committee law ("Committee for Safeguarding National Security of the Macao SAR", passed 19 March 2026): the Macao government release (S12) gives only the date and a general description. The closed-door, final-decision and lawyer-clearance details come from Reuters-based reporting and AFP/Arab News (S13, S11 search summary), not from the bill text.
- The IMF Macao Article IV 2026 press release (S19) says nothing on the peg or capital flows; IMF AREAER and the staff report were not opened. The capital position rests on the Basic Law (S6) and secondary sources.
- Not found / not opened, hence not asserted: any Macau asset-freeze power in national security cases; FATF/APG status after the 2017 APG evaluation (the GGRAsia page, HTTP 403, was seen only in a search summary); whether the PRC Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law has been added to Annex III of the Macau Basic Law; the Macau capital gains tax position; the current status of the non-resident stamp duty surcharge; Freedom House and WJP scores (neither covers Macau, see 2.3).

## 1. Capital controls

- Basic Law (S6, WIPO Lex copy, opened): art. 111 says the Region "shall pursue the policy of free trade and safeguard the free movement of goods, intangible assets and capital"; art. 108 says "The Macao Pataca, as the legal tender in the Macao Special Administrative Region, shall continue to circulate"; art. 106 "independent taxation system". Unlike Hong Kong art. 112, the pages opened do not show an explicit "no foreign exchange control" sentence (the fetch summary quoted arts. 108 and 111 only).
- NYU GlobaLex (S3): the pataca "continues to circulate and remains freely convertible". Macau investment agency IPIM (S5) was not opened (certificate error); a search summary only says Macau allows the free flow of capital and runs a currency board.
- IMF Article IV 2026 (S19, Board concluded 24 Sept 2026): says nothing about the peg or capital flows in the release; it recommends Macao "Sustain ongoing efforts to strengthen AML/CFT frameworks".
- Reading: no sign of capital controls on individuals. The legal guarantee is shorter than Hong Kong's, but practice is open (search summaries, not opened pages: AMCM assures full convertibility of the pataca into Hong Kong dollars).
- Countervailing: no asset-freeze power was found for Macau (see 3.3). It is not established either way, and absence of a documented power is not proof of absence.

## 2. Property rights, rule of law, courts

### 2.1 Property
- Basic Law art. 6 (S6): the Region "shall protect the right of private ownership of property in accordance with law"; art. 103 protects "the acquisition, use, disposal and inheritance of property". Both are conditioned by the phrase "in accordance with law". The compensation sentences of these articles were not quoted in what I opened.
- No restriction on who can buy: a property agent's 2015 article in Macau Daily Times (S8) says "there are no 'restrictions' on who can buy and who can't in Macau", and that in 2013 an additional 10% stamp duty was imposed on non-residents and companies. That is 2015; the Macao government release of 20 Dec 2025 (S10) describes only a resident stamp duty exemption (first MOP 6M of a residential purchase, from 1 January 2026) and does not mention a non-resident surcharge. Whether the 10% is still in force is not established.
- Land: much land is held under state concessions with a legal standard term of 25 years and renewals of up to 10 years, no automatic renewal, and no concession beyond 19 December 2049 yet granted (S18, Macau Business). Private land ownership of historical private land is protected by the Land Law (S18), but how that applies to apartments in practice was not explained. Hong Kong has an automated 50-year extension; Macau has no equivalent (S18).

### 2.2 Courts
- Basic Law (S6): arts. 83 and 89, courts act independently and judges decide according to law; art. 87, the Chief Executive appoints judges on the recommendation of an independent commission, and qualified foreign judges may be employed; art. 88, the Court of Final Appeal president must be a Chinese citizen and permanent resident.
- NYU GlobaLex (S3): three tiers, the Court of Final Appeal under Sam Hou Fai (who became Chief Executive in 2024) has final adjudication and there is no appeal to Beijing courts. (The GlobaLex page is dated; Sam Hou Fai's current job is from S11.)
- **Foreign judges and national security.** Macau Business (S4, 2 July 2018) reports a bill under which only Chinese judges would be appointed to hear national security cases; the Secretary for Administration and Justice said it "does not discriminate". Macau Daily Times (S17, 15 Feb 2019) says the government's final text "upheld the original proposal", which bans foreign judges from national security matters. That is a bill and a government text; I did not open the enacted law. Note that this was adopted in 2018-19, long before the 2023 amendment, and Macau has not had the overseas judge resignations seen in Hong Kong: nothing I opened reports foreign judges leaving Macau courts, and I did not find how many foreign judges sit there (not established).
- Commercial courts: no source opened evidences interference in ordinary commercial cases. No Macau equivalent to Hong Kong's Evergrande or Tiananmen vigil rulings was found. Absence of evidence.

### 2.3 Indices
- **WJP Rule of Law Index 2025: Macau is not covered.** I downloaded the 2025 report PDF (S1) and searched its text: zero occurrences of "Macau" or "Macao"; Hong Kong SAR, China appears (24/143, 0.72).
- **Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026: Macao is listed but "Not covered"** (S2, scores table: no score or status). Hong Kong is shown as 41/100 Partly Free on the same page. The Freedom House country page URL for Macao returned 404. So neither index can be used as a screen here.

### 2.4 Elections
- On 15 July 2025 the Electoral Affairs Commission disqualified 12 candidates (two lists, including incumbent Ron Lam) on an opinion letter from the Committee for Safeguarding National Security, chaired by the Chief Executive; the grounds were that they were "not supportive" and "not allegiant" and appeal was not possible (S16, Macau Business). The 2024 electoral law changes made the committee's assessment mandatory and binding (search summary, not opened). The Portugal Post (S21, 25 Sept 2026) says the 26th EU annual report cites this and the October 2025 closure of the outlet All About Macau (the EU report itself could not be opened; the Romanian mirror refused connection).

## 3. The Law on Safeguarding National Security (Law 2/2009, amended May 2023)

### 3.1 2009 text
- CECC copy of the 2009 English text (S9): art. 1 (treason) limited to "Any Chinese citizen"; arts. 2-5 use "Any person"; art. 10(2) extends reach outside Macau by category, for Macau residents; arts. 6-7 cover foreign political organisations and Macau groups linked to them. The only asset provisions in that text are fines and the use of an unincorporated body's property to pay a fine (art. 8), and loss of subsidies (art. 9(3)). No freezing power in the 2009 text (as summarised by the fetch tool, not read line by line).

### 3.2 2023 amendment (passed unanimously 18 May 2023, in force 30 May 2023)
- Secession now covers non-violent acts and "any illegal means"; subversion extended from the Central People's Government to "the political power ruling the nation"; new sedition limb on inciting riots; "theft of state secrets" renamed "violation of state secrets"; the "foreign political organisations" language is replaced by organisations outside Macau (S22 Macau Post Daily, S23 VOA, S24 Macau Business).
- **Nationality.** Macau Business (S24): for offences committed outside Macau, the law now applies to "any individual"; the previous version said "by residents". HRW (S7) says the 2023 amendments "extended extraterritorial jurisdiction to nonresidents" and added 30 articles. The Vibes (search summary, not opened) says the same. So the extraterritorial reach to any nationality is documented in two opened secondary sources, but I could not read the article text.
- Suspects can face communications surveillance and a ban on leaving Macau for up to five days on court orders (S25 The Vibes; also seen in HKFP via search summary only). Penalty maximum 25 years (search summary, a VOA/EU source, not verified); HRW says the Article 13 offence (links with outside groups) carries a maximum of 10 years (S7).
- Han Zhu's 2024 Hong Kong Law Journal abstract (S26) says the revised law drew "heavily on the 2020 Hong Kong National Security Law" and "deviated from its pro-liberal, narrowly defined precursor". Only the abstract was opened.
- Interception: a search summary describes a 2023 bill widening police interception to crimes endangering national security (up to three months a time, judge extension), enactment not verified. Not asserted.

### 3.3 Asset-freeze power
- **Not found.** The US HK Business Advisory (S15, opened as PDF) discusses Hong Kong freezing assets of suspects until proceedings end (removal of the two-year cap, Dec 2023) but contains **zero** occurrences of "Macau" or "Macao": it does not cover Macau. No opened source describes a Macau freeze, and a targeted search for it returned nothing for Macau (search summary). In the Au Kam San case (3.5) none of the reports I opened mentions assets. This limb of the definition is **unproven** for Macau.

### 3.4 2026 procedure law (Committee law)
- 19 March 2026: Legislative Assembly unanimously passed the bill "Committee for Safeguarding National Security of the Macao SAR" (S12, Macao government, date and general description only), in force the next day after gazette publication (about 24 March, S11 Arab News date 24 March).
- According to Reuters-based and AFP-based reports (S13 Big Lychee reproducing AFP/AP text: "A March bill lets Macau judges decide whether national security cases are heard in camera. Defence lawyers must also get clearance"; S11 Arab News): closed-door hearings possible; defence lawyers need clearance from national security officials; HRW (Maya Wang, S11) says "anyone who dares to criticize the government can be punished in the name of national security"; Macau lawyer Sergio de Almeida Correia: "Any judicial case, however innocuous, can now acquire the label of being sensitive". The statements that the committee has "ultimate authority", with "decisions final, not subject to appeal or judicial review", come from a search summary only (Reuters text, page not opened; S11 does not confirm).
- The 2026 Policy Address (RTP, S27) promises a separate law for the committee, a specific regime for the loyalty-vetting of election candidates, further counter-terrorism legislation and a review of the anti-money-laundering law. Date not shown on the page.

### 3.5 Cases
- **Au Kam San**, 68-69, former pro-democracy lawmaker, Portuguese citizen, arrested 30 July 2025; held in preventive detention ordered by the Criminal Instruction Court (S28 RTP, S7 HRW). Charge as reported by AP (S29, 29 July 2026): subversion of state power, breach of confidentiality, establishing contact with outside groups; the "principal offender" finding on 2 July 2026. Alleged conduct: Facebook posts and interviews and contact with overseas groups. The family could not speak to him for almost a year; the lawyer hired by the family was refused access; the court appointed a lawyer after a binding opinion from the security committee; the Portuguese consulate's request for access was refused (S29). The EU said denying judicial due process violates his rights (S29).
- First closed-door trial opened 16 September 2026 and ended 18 September; verdict "on a date to be scheduled later" (S30 The Star). The Portugal Post (S21, 25 Sept) says he remains in custody awaiting sentencing. I found no verdict as of today (search summary of recent reports, not conclusive).
- This is the **only known national security prosecution** in Macau since the law's 2009 enactment; the person is a local resident, a Portuguese-Macanese national, and the conduct was online criticism. The case shows lawyer vetting, committee involvement and consular denial. It does not involve a non-Chinese person living outside Macau, and no foreign resident has been prosecuted as far as I found.

## 4. Personal risk

- US State Department (second-hand): Macau is **Level 3, Reconsider Travel**, reissued 4/5 September 2026, one step higher than Hong Kong and mainland China (Level 2) (S14 Destination.com, S15b Fox News). Fox reports the reason as "similar concerns" to Hong Kong and the limited ability to provide emergency consular help, and says Macau is named in the warning that Americans could be detained or deported over private electronic messages "critical of China, Hong Kong, Macau or the Chinese Communist Party". Destination.com says the reissued statements did not detail Macau-specific threats beyond "some areas have increased risk". That is a puzzling Level 3 given the narrow wording; it should be re-checked on the official page. Second-hand: official page returned 403.
- UK FCDO Macao (S20 safety and security page, opened 2026-10-16; main page updated 11 December 2025): "You could face criminal charges if you take part in activities that the authorities believe threaten national security"; "This includes activities in and outside the Macao SAR, including in the UK"; "The police have the right to access an individual's social media and private communications". The page as fetched has no detention, removal-to-mainland or exit-ban wording (unlike FCDO's Hong Kong page, which says people "could be detained or removed to mainland China"). Macao does not recognise dual nationality (main page).
- Consular access to a dual national (Portuguese-Macau) was refused in the Au case (S29).

## 5. Sanctions and geopolitical exposure

- The Sept 2024 US Updated Hong Kong Business Advisory (S15) does not mention Macau at all. No OFAC/Treasury advisory on Macau was found. So the "documented by government advisories" element is met for Macau only by the general State Department travel advisory (Level 3) and the FCDO wording, not by a business or financial advisory.
- PRC Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law in Macau: **not established.** The 2021 plan to add national laws to Annex III of both Basic Laws was reported (S31 Asia Times, Oct 2021): the NPC Standing Committee "did not pass the scheduled motion on August 20" 2021. The page does not say what happened afterwards for Macau. Open question for counsel.

## 6. Other

- Tax (PwC, last reviewed 2 June 2026, S32): "There are no inheritance, estate, or gift taxes in Macau SAR"; "There are no net wealth/worth taxes"; professional tax on employment income in Macau with top rate 12%. The page does not address capital gains, dividends or interest, so those are not asserted.
- AML: IMF 2026 (S19) urges continued AML/CFT work. 2017 APG evaluation (GGRAsia, search summary only, page 403): six of eleven outcomes "substantial", two "low" (prosecution). No FATF listing found; a 2026 policy plan to study revising the AML law (S27). The casino/junket context is not assessed here.

## 7. Verdict

**Does Macau meet `security_state_courts`? Knock-out, meets it with weaker evidence than Hong Kong (borderline).** The regime and the procedure fit, the enforcement record and the financial-asset limb do not.

Evidence for:
1. Override of ordinary judicial protections: closed trials where court and committee decide (S11, S13, S30); defence lawyers need clearance from security officials, and the committee's binding opinion was used to replace a family-chosen lawyer (S29); foreign judges barred from national security cases (S4, S17, bill and government text); the Chief Executive chairs the committee whose opinion on candidates is binding and not appealable (S16). These are stronger than in Hong Kong on the lawyer-access point: the lawyer of choice was refused (S29).
2. Reach to any nationality: "any individual" for offences outside Macau after May 2023 (S24), non-residents (S7); FCDO says it covers activities "outside the Macao SAR, including in the UK" (S20); US warns of detention over private messages critical of China, Hong Kong, Macau (S15b).
3. Real use: Au Kam San held from July 2025 in preventive detention, closed trial in September 2026, no consular access for a dual national (S29, S30, S21).
4. Government advisory: US Level 3 (S14, S15b second-hand) and FCDO national security wording (S20).
5. Direction of travel: 2023 law drawing on the Hong Kong NSL (S26), 2024 electoral changes, 2026 Committee law, and a 2026 Policy Address promising more (S27).

Evidence against (what is missing):
1. **No asset-freeze power found.** The Hong Kong knock-out rests on freezing without time limit (S15); nothing for Macau in any opened source, and the 2009 text has only fines (S9). This limb of the definition is unproven.
2. **No Macau business or sanctions advisory.** The US Business Advisory has zero Macau mentions (S15). No sanctions on Macau officials found.
3. **One prosecution in 17 years, no conviction yet, no foreigner resident prosecuted.** Hong Kong has 400+ arrests and 180+ convictions (S29 context line). The Macau record is a single resident who is a local Portuguese national and an activist.
4. **No overseas-judge exodus and no sign of interference in commercial cases** (absence of evidence, not proof).
5. **Capital is free in law and practice, no inheritance or wealth tax** (S6, S32); the US advisory language on Macau is thin (Destination.com reports no Macau-specific threats) and FCDO shows no detention/removal wording.
6. Neither WJP nor Freedom House covers Macau (S1, S2), so there is no index figure like Hong Kong's 41/100.
7. Several key facts (amended article numbers, the committee's finality, nationality of the extraterritorial limb) rest on news agencies, HRW and a search summary rather than on the statute text.

Recommended treatment: model it as `security_state_courts` with a reason string that states the evidence is thinner than Hong Kong, or, if the model requires every limb of the definition (asset freeze), as a red flag with the reason string below. The strict-definition gap is the asset freeze; the closest analogue (committee-controlled lawyer access and closed trials) is stronger than Hong Kong's.
